Model Answers — annotated against descriptors

Each paragraph annotated for which descriptor it earns marks against. Originals written for this guide. Do not memorise — analyse, then adapt your own ideas.

1. Task 2 Opinion essay — Band 7.5 target

Prompt

Some people believe that governments should invest more in public transport rather than building new roads. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Intro — As congestion and emissions worsen in many cities, the question of whether public funds are best spent on expanding public transport or constructing additional roads has become increasingly pressing. I largely agree with the view that investment should favour public transport, although a measured commitment to road maintenance remains necessary.

TR: paraphrases prompt, clear position (largely agree) with nuance. LR: "pressing", "measured commitment". GRA: complex sentence with subordinate clause + adverbial opening.

Body 1 — The strongest argument for prioritising public transport is its capacity to ease congestion at scale. A single underground or light-rail line can carry the equivalent of several lanes of traffic, which means that each pound spent on transit yields a far greater reduction in commute times than the same sum poured into widening a motorway. London's Elizabeth line, for example, has shifted millions of daily journeys off road, demonstrably easing pressure on parallel routes. Building more roads, conversely, often triggers induced demand: new lanes fill with new cars within a few years.

TR: main idea extended with reasoning (per-£ comparison) + example (Elizabeth line) + counter-mechanism (induced demand). CC: clear topic sentence; connectors used sparingly ("conversely" once). LR: collocation "ease congestion", "induced demand". GRA: relative clause ("which means"), passive ("has shifted").

Body 2 — Beyond traffic, public transport investment also delivers environmental and social dividends that roads cannot match. Trains and buses emit far less CO₂ per passenger kilometre than private cars, helping cities meet climate targets that would otherwise drift out of reach. Equally important is the equity argument: those who cannot afford a car — students, low-income workers, the elderly — depend on transit to access employment and services. A reliable bus network, in other words, is not merely an environmental measure but a tool for inclusion. That said, some road investment is still warranted to maintain freight corridors and emergency access; the choice is one of emphasis, not exclusion.

TR: second main idea (environment + equity), extended; concession kept (nuance = Band 7+ TR). CC: paragraph progresses environment → equity → concession. LR: "dividends", "drift out of reach", "freight corridors". GRA: variety — cleft ("the choice is one of"), conditional ("would otherwise"), participle clause ("helping cities meet…").

Conclusion — On balance, governments should direct the greater share of transport budgets to public systems, since these deliver larger gains in congestion, emissions and equity than equivalent road spending. Roads will continue to matter, but only as the supporting cast in a transit-led strategy.

TR: restates position with reasons. LR: "supporting cast in a transit-led strategy" (precise metaphor). GRA: complex sentence with subordinate clause + comparison.

Word count: ~310. Bands estimated: TR 8 · CC 7 · LR 7.5 · GRA 7.5 → overall ~7.5.

2. Task 2 Discussion essay — Band 7+ target

Prompt

Some people believe young people should follow the traditions of their society. Others think they should be free to behave as individuals. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Intro — Whether young adults ought to honour the customs they inherit or chart an independent course is a long-standing tension. Both positions have merit, but I lean towards the view that individuality, exercised with respect for tradition, produces the healthiest outcome.

TR: paraphrases + previews both sides + signals stance. CC: tight intro. LR: "long-standing tension", "chart an independent course".

Body 1 — Defenders of tradition argue that customs provide young people with identity, continuity and a tested moral framework. Practices such as religious ceremonies, family rituals or even simple etiquette transmit values that have endured because they work — they bind communities together and reduce the rootlessness that often accompanies rapid change. In societies where traditional ties have eroded quickly, rising loneliness among the young is frequently cited as evidence that something has been lost.

TR: fair coverage of the "follow tradition" view, with reasoning + supporting observation. LR: "tested moral framework", "rootlessness". GRA: relative clause, present perfect ("have endured").

Body 2 — On the other hand, advocates of individuality point out that uncritical adherence to tradition can entrench inequality and stifle innovation. Many of the rights now taken for granted — gender equality, religious freedom, the choice of partner — were won precisely because young people refused to follow the script. Moreover, modern economies reward creativity and risk-taking, qualities that thrive when young adults are encouraged to question, not merely conform. My own view aligns more closely with this position: a society that cannot tolerate dissent from its norms will eventually pay for it in talent and progress.

TR: second view + extended example + own opinion signalled inside paragraph (acceptable). LR: "entrench inequality", "stifle innovation", "talent and progress". GRA: cleft ("were won precisely because"), participle ("qualities that thrive when…").

Conclusion — In short, traditions deserve thoughtful engagement, not blind allegiance. Young people are likely to flourish when they can draw on inherited wisdom while reserving the right to revise it where it falls short — and societies that allow this dual stance are the ones that endure.

TR: restates balance + stance. LR: "blind allegiance", "inherited wisdom". GRA: subordinate clauses, comparison structure.

3. GT Task 1 — Formal letter (Band 7+)

Prompt

You bought a new smartphone online but it arrived damaged. Write a letter to the company. Explain the situation; describe the damage; say what you want them to do.

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing to formally complain about a smartphone I purchased from your online store on 12 May, which arrived in a damaged condition.

Opening: purpose stated in 1 sentence, formal register, includes order date. TR: situation introduced.

The order, reference number A-44219, was delivered last Friday. When I opened the package, I noticed that the screen had a long crack running across its lower half and that the rear casing was visibly dented. The box itself appeared intact, which suggests the damage occurred before dispatch rather than in transit. I have kept the packaging and taken photographs, which I can provide on request.

Bullet 1 (situation) + Bullet 2 (damage described): specific detail = TR fulfilment. LR: "running across", "visibly dented", "intact". GRA: relative clause ("which suggests"), past simple + past participle mix.

Given that the device is unusable and the fault is clearly not mine, I would be grateful if you could arrange a full replacement of the same model, dispatched at no additional cost. If a replacement is not feasible within seven working days, I would prefer a complete refund to the original payment method.

Bullet 3 (action) with a clear alternative. LR: "dispatched at no additional cost". GRA: conditional structure, hedged tone ("would prefer", "would be grateful").

I look forward to your prompt response and a swift resolution to this matter.

Yours faithfully,
[Your Name]

Closing: formal sign-off, polite forward-looking close.

4. GT Task 1 — Informal letter (Band 7+)

Prompt

A friend is visiting your country next month. Write a letter telling them: when to come; what to bring; what you will do together.

Hi Maya,

I was so happy to hear you're finally coming over next month — I've been looking forward to this for ages.

If you can, try to arrive in the second half of June. The weather will be warmer by then and the rains usually hold off until July, so we'll get the best of the long evenings. I'll have a few days off around the 20th, which is when I'd love to take you around properly.

As for what to pack: light clothes mostly, a good pair of walking shoes, and a thin jacket for the evenings — it cools down more than you'd expect once the sun goes. Don't bother bringing a hairdryer or adapters, I've got spares.

I've got a few things lined up. We'll spend a day in the old town, climb up to the hill temple at sunrise (worth the early start, trust me), and I want to take you to the night market — the street food alone is a reason to visit. If you're up for it, we can also drive out to the coast for a weekend.

Let me know your flight details once you've booked, and I'll come pick you up.

Can't wait!
Sam

Informal tone throughout: contractions ("you're", "I've"), phrasal verbs ("hold off", "pick up"), idiomatic ("worth the early start, trust me"). All 3 bullets covered, varied sentence length, natural cohesion via reference and topic-comment progression rather than connector-spam.

5. Academic Task 1 — Bar chart description (Band 7+)

Prompt (described)

The chart below shows the percentage of households in three regions (A, B, C) with broadband internet access in 2005, 2015 and 2024. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

The bar chart compares broadband internet penetration across three regions — A, B and C — at three points in time: 2005, 2015 and 2024.

Intro: paraphrases prompt, no copy.

Overall, broadband access expanded substantially in all three regions over the two decades, but the pace of growth differed sharply: Region A led throughout, Region C closed most of the gap, while Region B remained the slowest to adopt.

Overview: 2 big trends (universal growth + diverging pace) without specific figures. Mandatory paragraph, often missed.

In 2005, only about 18% of households in Region A had broadband, compared with 9% in B and a mere 3% in C. By 2015, Region A had more than tripled this figure to roughly 62%, while Region B reached 41% and Region C climbed to 24%. The largest leap occurred between 2015 and 2024 for Region C, which surged from 24% to around 78%, narrowing the gap with Region A (now at 91%) to just over ten percentage points. Region B grew more modestly, ending the period at 70%.

Detail paragraph: specific figures, comparison language, "tripled", "surged", "narrowing the gap". GRA: past simple + past perfect, comparative structures.

By the end of the period, broadband had become near-universal in Region A and was approaching the same status in C, whereas B, despite its earlier head start over C, fell behind in relative terms.

Brief closing comparison sharpens the trend; no opinion, no speculation.
