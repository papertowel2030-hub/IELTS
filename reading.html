Reading

40 questions in 60 minutes on screen, and no extra time at the end — when the clock stops, your answers are final. Content below adapts to your test (toggle Academic/General in the sidebar). Then apply everything in the Reading Practice sets.

Rough targets — General: ~34/40 → Band 7 · ~37/40 → Band 8.Academic: ~30/40 → Band 7 · ~35/40 → Band 8. Same question types either way; the difference is the texts.

General Training: three parts that get harder — everyday notices/ads, then work-related texts, then one longer general-interest passage.

Academic: three long passages (700–900 words each) of roughly equal difficulty, from journals, books and magazines.

Time budget (General): Section 1 ≈ 17 min · Section 2 ≈ 18 min · Section 3 ≈ 23 min. The hardest section gets the most time — not equal time.
Time budget (Academic): ~20 min per passage, since they're equal difficulty. Bank spare time on the passage you find easiest.
The #1 time-killer: leaving questions "for later" without marking them. Answer as you go, and tap Review (the flag) on any question you're unsure about — the review screen at the bottom shows exactly which ones are flagged or empty.
Use the computer's tools — they replace your pencil: select text and right-click to highlight keywords or add a note to a paragraph. The passage and questions sit side-by-side, and the timer counts down at the top (it turns red in the last 10 minutes). Practise with these tools on so they're automatic on test day.

Everyone says "skim and scan". For Band 7+ that isn't enough. The real skill is spotting that a word in the question has been swapped for a synonym in the passage.

Question says "increase" → the passage might say rise, growth, upward trend, surge, climb, jump. Your job is to recognise they mean the same thing.
Paraphrase drill (daily): take any practice passage + questions. For each question, find the matching sentence and highlight both the question word and its paraphrase in the text (right-click → highlight, like the real test). After ~20, you start predicting the patterns.
Apply

Question: "The scheme was abandoned due to cost." Which passage phrase is the paraphrase to hunt for?

"abandoned" → "scrapped", and "due to cost" → "because it proved too costly". Matching meaning, not matching words, is the whole game.

Build your own paraphrase set. 4 ways a passage could say "important" without the word — then 4 for "increase".

important → crucial · vital · plays a central role · significant.
increase → rise · surge · climb · upward trend.

Don't do questions top-to-bottom. Do them in the smart order, because each type needs a different move:

TypeWhen to do itTactic
Matching HeadingsFirst (if present)Get paragraph gist before micro-questions spoil your fresh read.
True / False / Not GivenAfter headingsFind the sentence, then judge (see next lesson).
Yes / No / Not GivenSameSame logic, but about the writer's opinion, not facts.
Sentence / summary completionMiddlePredict word class. Answer is usually word-for-word from the text.
Multiple choiceMiddleEliminate 2 wrong options first. Trap = a true statement that doesn't answer the question.
Matching information to paragraphsLast (slow)Scan for unique nouns/names. These take the most time.

This type sinks more scores than any other. Use the same 3-way test every time:

1. Find the sentence(s) about the question's topic.
2. Compare the claim in the question to what the passage says.
3. Decide: does the passage support it (→ TRUE), contradict it (→ FALSE), or stay silent (→ NOT GIVEN)?
Quantifier trap: if the question says "all/always X" but the passage says "some/often X", that's a contradiction → FALSE, not Not Given.
The passage supports the claim →TRUE
The passage contradicts the claim →FALSE
The passage says nothing about the claim →NOT GIVEN — silence, not contradiction. Never fill the gap with your own knowledge.
Question says "all/always", passage says "some/often" →FALSE. A weaker quantifier contradicts a universal claim — this is the trap most people call Not Given.
Apply

Passage: "The library opens at 9am on weekdays." Statement: "The library is open every day of the week." → ?

The passage only mentions weekdays. It says nothing about weekends — so we can't confirm or deny "every day". Silence = Not Given. (If it had said "closed at weekends", that would be False.)

Write your own Not Given pair. One passage sentence + one statement the passage stays silent on. If you can build the trap, you can spot it.

Passage: "The museum opens at 10am on Saturdays." · Statement: "Entry to the museum is free on Saturdays." → Not Given — the passage says nothing about price. ("The museum is closed on Saturdays" would be False.)

For headings, "read the questions first" actually backfires — the heading list primes you toward the wrong fit. Do the opposite:

1. Don't read the heading list yet — keep your eyes off that panel.
2. Skim paragraph A — first two sentences + the last sentence. Type its gist in 3–5 words as an on-screen note on the paragraph (right-click → note).
3. Repeat for B, C, D…
4. Now read the headings and match them to your own notes. Eliminate as you go.
Myth: a heading that repeats a word from the paragraph is the answer. Truth: that's often the trap. Match the paragraph's main point, not a shared keyword.
