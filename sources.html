Research & Sources

Every method in this guide is built on peer-reviewed cognitive science or the official IELTS rubric — not opinion. Here are the primary sources, with links. If a claim in the guide has a small number next to it, it points here.

How learning actually sticks

1 · Retrieval practice

Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249–255.

Testing yourself beats re-reading. After a week's delay, students who self-tested remembered far more than students who simply re-studied — even with no feedback. This is why the guide makes you recall and quiz, not just read.

🔗 journals.sagepub.com — doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x

2 · Spaced repetition

Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed Practice in Verbal Recall Tasks: A Review and Quantitative Synthesis. Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354–380.

A meta-analysis of 317 experiments: spreading study out over time beats cramming for long-term memory. This is why the guide uses short daily reviews and flashcards instead of marathon sessions.

🔗 pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566

3 · Interleaving

Taylor, K., & Rohrer, D. (2010). The Effect of Interleaving Practice. Applied Cognitive Psychology, 24(6), 837–848.

Mixing problem types ("interleaving") felt harder during practice but doubled test scores a day later, because learners got better at choosing the right approach. This is why you rotate all four skills through the week instead of studying one at a time.

🔗 digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1760

4 · Desirable difficulties

Bjork, E. L., & Bjork, R. A. (2011). Making Things Hard on Yourself, but in a Good Way: Creating Desirable Difficulties to Enhance Learning. In Psychology and the Real World.

Conditions that make practice feel harder (testing, spacing, mixing) produce stronger, longer-lasting learning. Struggling a little is the point — not a sign you're failing.

🔗 bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu (PDF)

Why we keep it small & calm

5 · Cognitive load

Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning. Cognitive Science, 12(2), 257–285.

Working memory is easily overloaded; when it is, learning stops. Good instruction removes clutter ("extraneous load"). This is why lessons here are one idea at a time, with the detail hidden until you need it.

🔗 sciencedirect.com — 0364021388900237

6 · Working-memory limits

Cowan, N. (2001). The Magical Number 4 in Short-Term Memory: A Reconsideration of Mental Storage Capacity. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 24(1), 87–185.

Most people hold only about four chunks of new information at once. So the guide chunks everything into small, named pieces (a "decision tree", a "5-step routine") rather than long lists to memorise.

🔗 cambridge.org — Behavioral and Brain Sciences

7 · ADHD & executive function

Barkley, R. A. (1997). Behavioral Inhibition, Sustained Attention, and Executive Functions: Constructing a Unifying Theory of ADHD. Psychological Bulletin, 121(1), 65–94.

ADHD is best understood as a challenge with self-regulation and working memory — not effort or intelligence. The fix is external structure: visible next-steps, timers, checklists, and progress you can see. That is exactly how this guide is built.

🔗 Psychological Bulletin (PDF)

8 · Generation effect

Slamecka, N. J., & Graf, P. (1978). The Generation Effect: Delineation of a Phenomenon. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory, 4(6), 592–604.

Information you generate yourself is remembered substantially better than information you read. This is why every lesson ends with a "③ Make it yours" box asking for your own example, trap or sentence — the slowest-feeling step is the strongest one.

🔗 doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592
