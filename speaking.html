Even on the computer test, Speaking stays human: it's a live, face-to-face conversation with a real examiner (sometimes via video call), booked around your main test. No typing here — which is exactly why it needs live practice.
Myth: "Fill the full 2 minutes in Part 2 or you lose marks." False — the examiner stops you at 2 min anyway. Strong content that finishes naturally at 1:30 beats 2:00 of padding.
Myth: "Speak fast to sound fluent." Fast + unclear lowers both Fluency and Pronunciation. Fluency means smooth and paced, not rushed.
Myth: "Memorise ideal answers." The examiner deliberately varies questions — a memorised script crashes on contact.

Equal weight, 25% each. Know what "Band 7" sounds like for each:

Fluency & Coherence — you speak at length without much effort; any hesitation is you searching for an idea, not the words.
Lexical Resource — flexible vocabulary across topics, some less-common words, and you can paraphrase when you don't know a word.
Grammar — a range of complex structures, with frequent error-free sentences.
Pronunciation — generally easy to understand; your accent has only a limited effect (see next lesson — this is the one people neglect).

Most people only worry about individual sounds. Band 7+ is really about the music of English — the suprasegmentals:

Sentence stress — stress the content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives); glide over the small words.
Weak forms — in natural speech, for → /fə/, to → /tə/, and → /ən/. Stressing every word sounds robotic.
Chunking — group words into 4–7 word thought-units; pause at the boundaries, not mid-phrase.
Intonation — voice falls on statements and finished lists; rises on yes/no questions and unfinished thoughts.

Solo drills you can do today:

Minimal pairs — record ship/sheep, bit/beat, full/fool. Can you hear your own difference? · Shadowing — repeat a 60-sec BBC clip 1 sec behind, copying stress. · Chunk & mark — print a transcript, mark "/" for pauses and CAPS for stress, then read to your marks.
Accent ≠ pronunciation. You keep your accent — you're marked on stress, rhythm and intonation, not on sounding native.
Aim for 2–3 sentences. Example — "Do I like cooking? Yes, I'm really into it; I find chopping vegetables almost meditative after a long day. I mostly cook Asian food, since that's what I grew up with."
Vary your openings — don't start every answer with "Well…" or "I think…".
Don't over-extend. Part 1 is a warm-up; save your energy and best vocabulary for Parts 2 and 3.
Show a little grammar range early — the examiner forms a first impression fast.

The four bullets are scaffolding, not a script. In your prep minute, write keywords only:

Bullet 1: ____ Bullet 2: ____ Bullet 3: ____ Bullet 4: ____ + 1 sensory detail (a smell / sound / image) + 1 emotion word + 1 reflection ("looking back…")

For an event/experience card, tell it as a story with STAR-R:

Situation → Task/trigger → Action → Result → Reflection (how you feel about it now — this last one lifts your band).

For an object/place/person card: Past (first encounter) → Present (what it's like now) → Sensory detail → Why it matters.

Apply

In Part 2, which choice scores best?

Examiners mark content and language, not the clock. Covering the bullets plus a reflection, then a clean finish, beats both filler and a too-short answer.

Your turn — cue card: "Describe a place you love to visit." Do the 1-minute prep here: keywords for the 4 bullets (where / when you go / what you do / why you love it), plus one sensory detail, one emotion word, one reflection. Then say it out loud from your notes and time yourself.

Bullets: grandmother's rooftop garden · Fridays · watering, tea, talking · feels like childhood. Sensory: smell of wet soil after watering. Emotion: calm, grounded. Reflection: looking back, it's less about the plants and more about the ritual.

Part 3 is abstract discussion. Don't list points — develop one with Point → Reason → Example → Qualifier:

P: Technology has changed family life a lot. R: Because phones compete for attention even when people are together. E: At dinner, it's now normal to see everyone scrolling instead of talking. Q: That said, video calls do keep migrant families close — so it's not all bad.
Hedge naturally — "it tends to…", "generally speaking…", "it's likely that…". Nuance scores.
A 2-second pause is fine — "Hmm, that's a tough one, let me think." It's human, not a fluency drop.
Never say "I don't know." Speculate instead: "I haven't thought much about this, but I'd guess…".

Your turn — Part 3 question: "Should young children have smartphones?" Write your own P·R·E·Q: Point, Reason, Example, Qualifier. Then deliver it out loud without reading.

P: I don't think primary-school children need their own phones. R: They're too easy a distraction at an age when attention is still developing. E: Teachers here now collect phones at the door because lessons kept stalling. Q: That said, a simple phone for safety on the commute is fair enough.

Between live sessions, run this solo loop to prepare:

1. Grab a cue card (IELTS Liz Part 2 topics or Cambridge 17–19).
2. 1-min prep — keywords + sensory + emotion + reflection.
3. Record yourself on your phone. Aim ~90 seconds, cover all bullets.
4. Replay and self-check: count long hesitations, repeated filler words ("good/nice/thing"), and flat/monotone stretches. Log the pattern in the Error Log.
5. Re-record the same topic fixing your top 2 issues. Feel the difference.
