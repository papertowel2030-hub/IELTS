Speaking — Target Band 7.5

11–14 min face-to-face interview, recorded. 3 parts. Examiner is neutral. Four criteria, equal weight: Fluency & Coherence · Lexical Resource · Grammatical Range & Accuracy · Pronunciation.
Myth (delete from your brain): "Speak the full 1–2 minutes in Part 2 or your band drops." False. The examiner stops you at 2 min regardless. A natural finish at 1:30 with strong content beats 2:00 of filler. Examiners reward content and language, not duration.
Myth: "Use 'In my view…' / 'It depends on…' as transitions." These are on examiner-flagged memorised-phrase lists. Use them sparingly and only when they fit.
Myth: "Speak fast to sound fluent." Fluency = smooth + paced. Fast + unclear = Pronunciation drop AND Fluency drop.

Pronunciation — the most under-trained criterion

Most candidates focus only on individual sounds. Band 7+ needs the suprasegmentals:

  • Sentence stress — content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives) stressed; function words (articles, prepositions) unstressed.
  • Weak forms — for sounds /fə/, to sounds /tə/, and sounds /ən/ in normal speech. Speaking every word fully stressed = robotic.
  • Connected speech — linking ("an_apple"), elision ("nex(t)_week"), assimilation ("ten_pounds" → "tem pounds").
  • Chunking — group words into thought units of 4–7 words, pause at the chunk boundary, not mid-phrase.
  • Intonation — falling for statements / completed lists / wh-questions; rising for yes/no questions / unfinished thought / politeness.

Pron drills

Minimal pairs — record yourself saying ship/sheep, bit/beat, full/fool, cat/cut. Replay; can you hear the difference?
Shadowing — pick a 60-sec BBC clip. Repeat 1 sec behind. Match stress + intonation, not just words.
Chunk & mark — print a transcript. Mark / for chunk pauses, CAPITALS for stressed words. Read aloud to the marking.

Part 1 — strategy

  • Aim 2–3 sentences. "Do I like cooking? Yes, I'm into it — I find chopping vegetables almost meditative after a long day. I cook mostly Asian dishes because that's what I grew up with."
  • Vary structure: don't start every answer with "Well…" or "I think…".
  • Don't over-extend. P1 is warm-up; saving energy and lexis for P2/3 is strategic.
  • Show grammar range early — examiner forms initial impression in the first 60 seconds.

Part 2 — cue card, with narrative framework

The bullets are scaffolding, not a script. Use a narrative arc.

Prep minute (write KEYWORDS only, max 8 words)

Bullet 1: ____ Bullet 2: ____ Bullet 3: ____ Bullet 4: ____ + 1 sensory detail (smell, sound, image) + 1 emotion word + 1 reflection ("looking back…")

STAR-R framework for narrative cue cards (describe an event / time / experience)

  • Situation — when, where, who.
  • Task / Trigger — what happened, what you decided to do.
  • Action — what you did, step by step.
  • Result — outcome, what you took away.
  • Reflection — how you feel about it now, what it taught you. (← bumps band)

For object / place / person cue cards

  • Past: first encounter / origin.
  • Present: what it looks like / does / means now.
  • Sensory: what you see, hear, smell, feel about it.
  • Future / why-it-matters: projection or significance.

Closing the answer

Finish on a clean sentence. If the examiner does not stop you and you've covered all bullets, a soft close is fine: "…and that's pretty much what I remember about it." No filler-padding.

Part 3 — discussion of abstract ideas

  • Develop, don't list. Use Point → Reason → Example → Qualifier.
    P: I'd say technology has changed family life significantly. R: Because phones compete for attention even when families are physically together. E: Take dinner time — it's now common to see everyone scrolling instead of talking. Q: That said, video calls do help families separated by migration stay close, so the effect isn't all negative.
  • Use hedging naturally: "It tends to…", "It's likely that…", "Generally speaking…". Examiners reward nuance.
  • It's OK to say "Hmm, that's a tough one — let me think for a second." A 2-sec pause is human, not a Fluency drop.
  • Avoid memorised P3 templates. The examiner deviates the question deliberately to test flexibility.

Daily routine (15 min weekday / 60 min weekend)

Step 1: Cue card from IELTS Liz — Part 2 topics or Cambridge IELTS 17–19.
Step 2: 1 min prep — write keywords + sensory + emotion + reflection.
Step 3: Record yourself. Speak 90 sec target (NOT "full 2 min"). Cover all bullets.
Step 4: Replay. Self-score against descriptors. Count: hesitations / repeated words / unclear chunks. Log to error log.
Step 5: Re-record same topic, fix the top 2 issues. Notice the improvement gap.
Weekly: Submit one full mock (P1+P2+P3) for external feedback. See feedback paths.

Common mistakes

  • One-word P1 answers (or, opposite: 60-sec P1 answers eating P3 time).
  • Filler padding in P2 ("yeah, and so basically, yeah…") to hit 2 min.
  • Memorised "ideal answers" — examiner deviates the question and you crash.
  • Speaking too fast when nervous → pron drops, examiner asks to repeat.
  • Basic vocab loop ("good, nice, bad, happy") instead of precision.
  • Saying "I don't know" in P3 instead of speculating: "I haven't thought much about this, but I'd guess…"
  • Apologising for your English mid-answer — adds nothing, costs FC.
